We don't need to be Fastenal to compete against Fastenal

  • Home
  • Blog
  • Competing against the giants
September 1, 2026 IMS Global 5 min read

What we have learned trying to grow as an industrial SME in a market dominated by large corporations.

A few years ago I thought that in order to compete against companies like Grainger or Fastenal we had to look more and more like them. More products. More brands. More inventory. Better prices. For a long time, that way of thinking helped us grow.

If a customer asked us for something, we found a way to get it. If a new category appeared, we added it. And when we were competing against a larger company, our first reaction was often to lower the price.

Until we started asking ourselves something:

Does it really make sense for an SME to compete against a corporation by doing exactly the same thing they do?

I'm more and more convinced that it doesn't.

Competing on price alone has a limit

Those of us in industrial supply know this story.

A request for quote comes in. You look for the product, negotiate with manufacturers, calculate logistics, importation and lead time, and you present your best proposal. Then you find out who you are competing against: Grainger, Fastenal, some national distributor, or even the manufacturer directly.

And the question shows up: "How much lower can I go?"

We have made that mistake too, and honestly we still make it. We have cut margin to win an order, and afterwards you realize that yes, you won the sale, but you didn't necessarily do good business.

Because if your main advantage was being 5% cheaper, the day someone shows up 6% cheaper you will probably lose the customer.

There are things we simply cannot compete on. You also have to be realistic. An SME does not have the buying power, the infrastructure, the inventory or the resources of a global corporation.

Trying to compete head-on against that would be naive. But we don't need to either. The question we have started asking ourselves is a different one:

What can we do that an organization of that size can hardly do with our same speed and flexibility?

That's where the conversation changes.

We have seen it happen: a customer needs a product tomorrow. Their usual supplier is out of stock. They need to source something in the United States and bring it into Mexico. They need a special part. Or they simply have a problem and need someone to help them solve it.

In those moments the customer doesn't necessarily need the biggest supplier. They need the supplier who solves it — and that is where a well-structured SME can be extraordinarily competitive.

The risk of wanting to sell everything

This has also been an important lesson for us. In trying to solve our customers' needs we started expanding categories. That has an advantage: you develop an enormous sourcing capability. But it also carries a risk.

Wanting to sell everything and ending up a specialist in nothing.

The answer we have found has not been to stop selling products. It has been to stop defining ourselves by them — because a glove, a tool or an abrasive can probably be bought from many suppliers. The important question is:

What value are we creating around that product?

  • Knowing the customer's consumption patterns.
  • Holding strategic inventory and anticipating replenishment.
  • Managing minimums and maximums.
  • Solving an emergency and finding hard-to-source products.
  • Reducing lead times and using technology.
  • Helping the customer simplify their supply chain.

At that point we are no longer talking only about selling a box or a product. We are talking about solving supply.

From winning an order to winning a relationship

We have also changed the way we understand a sale.

The question used to be:

How do we win this quote?

Today I find it far more interesting to ask ourselves:

How do we become a supplier this customer wants to keep for the next ten years?

Because an order can be won on price. A relationship of years is won by building trust — and when you know the customer's operation, understand their consumption, hold inventory and respond when there is a problem, you start building something much harder than a sale. A relationship.

Being hard to replace

We are still building this model and we will surely keep making mistakes. But it is increasingly clear to me that our growth cannot depend on undercutting another supplier by a few pesos. It has to depend on how much value we are able to create around what we sell.

If a customer only remembers us because we were the cheapest, we are probably easy to replace. If they remember us because we solved it when they had a problem, because we know their operation and because we constantly look for ways to make their supply more efficient, then we are building something different.

An industrial SME may be small next to a global corporation. But it can be enormously important to a customer's operation. We don't need to become Grainger or Fastenal.

We need to find what we can do extraordinarily well and build our company around it. Not competing to be the biggest. Competing to be indispensable.
